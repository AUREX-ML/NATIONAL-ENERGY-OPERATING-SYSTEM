## 1. Programme Purpose

The National Energy Operating System Programme exists to develop and validate the digital, institutional, operational, and governance foundations required to coordinate distributed energy resources across facilities, regional systems, and national energy-sector functions.

The programme addresses the coordination gap created by increasing distributed generation, storage, electric mobility, net metering, open-access arrangements, evolving energy markets, smart metering, and multi-institutional energy data flows.

## 2. Programme Vision

Enable Kenya to operate distributed energy resources as a coordinated, secure, interoperable, and evidence-based energy ecosystem while preserving legitimate institutional authority, local safety, asset-owner rights, and public-interest governance.

## 3. Programme Mission

Design, validate, and progressively implement an open digital coordination layer that allows authorized energy-sector stakeholders to observe, coordinate, verify, and learn from distributed energy resources across facility, regional, and national levels.

## 4. Strategic Objectives

The programme should:

1. Establish a validated national problem definition for distributed energy coordination.
2. Develop a staged technical architecture from Facility EMS to Regional DERMS to National DERMS.
3. Define institutional roles, authority boundaries, and governance requirements.
4. Prove facility-level coordination through controlled pilots.
5. Establish evidence standards for operational value, safety, reliability, cybersecurity, and economic impact.
6. Prepare a pathway for regional coordination once facility capability is proven.
7. Support future national coordination only after legal, institutional, technical, financial, and operational readiness is demonstrated.

## 5. Scope

### In Scope

- Facility Energy Management System architecture and pilots.
- Regional DERMS architecture and readiness pathway.
- National DERMS concept and federation model.
- Data, interoperability, identity, telemetry, and digital-twin architecture.
- Cybersecurity, privacy, resilience, and audit requirements.
- Governance, risk, compliance, assurance, and evidence systems.
- Stakeholder, institutional, regulatory, and market-interface mapping.
- Pilot design, measurement, and readiness criteria.
- Open standards and open-source strategy where appropriate.

### Out of Scope at Current Stage

- Claiming national deployment or production operation.
- Assuming regulatory approval or statutory mandate.
- Operating as a utility, regulator, system operator, market operator, retailer, generator, or asset owner.
- Unrestricted remote control of energy assets.
- Unvalidated peer-to-peer electricity trading.
- National procurement before discovery, pilots, and evidence.
- Fixed pricing or financial forecasts without validated unit economics.

## 6. Programme Architecture

The programme scales by proving capability at progressively larger levels:

```text
Laboratory
  ↓
Facility EMS
  ↓
Portfolio Aggregation
  ↓
Regional DERMS
  ↓
National DERMS
  ↓
National Energy Operating System
```

Each level must preserve the principle:

> Coordinate globally; control locally.

## 7. Stakeholder Groups

Potential stakeholder groups include:

- Ministry and national policy institutions;
- energy regulator;
- transmission and distribution utilities;
- system operation and control-centre functions;
- county governments where relevant;
- commercial and industrial facilities;
- mini-grid and distribution-system operators;
- renewable-energy, storage, and EV-charging operators;
- aggregators and energy-service companies;
- equipment manufacturers and system integrators;
- universities and research institutions;
- cybersecurity and data-protection authorities;
- development-finance institutions and strategic funders;
- consumers and asset owners.

Stakeholder participation does not automatically create decision rights. Decision rights must follow legal mandate, contractual authority, delegated responsibility, and governance design.

## 8. Governance Principles

The programme shall follow these governance principles:

- Authority is explicit, bounded, auditable, and reviewable.
- Safety and physical protection override optimization.
- Asset-owner consent and rights are respected.
- Cybersecurity is a foundational requirement.
- Data access follows purpose, authorization, minimization, retention, and audit rules.
- Material claims require evidence.
- Pilots must include clear baselines and acceptance criteria.
- National-scale readiness requires institutional readiness, not only technical capability.

## 9. Delivery Workstreams

Defines government, utility, regulator, research, technology, implementation, and development-finance participation models.

## 12. Decision Gates

| Gate | Question | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Gate 0 | Is the problem statement validated by relevant stakeholders? | Proceed to pilot design or revise scope. |
| Gate 1 | Is a facility pilot technically and institutionally feasible? | Proceed to pilot implementation planning or close gaps. |
| Gate 2 | Did the pilot prove measurable value and safe operation? | Productize Facility EMS or redesign. |
| Gate 3 | Can multiple facilities be coordinated without losing local autonomy? | Proceed to portfolio aggregation. |
| Gate 4 | Is regional coordination legally, technically, and operationally feasible? | Proceed to regional prototype or continue research. |
| Gate 5 | Is national coordination justified by evidence and mandate? | Prepare national programme design. |

## 13. Success Criteria

The programme is successful at the current stage when it can:

- explain the national coordination problem clearly;
- distinguish proposed capability from existing institutional authority;
- identify appropriate pilot pathways;
- define evidence required for further decisions;
- attract serious stakeholder review without overstating maturity;
- create a credible path from local proof to national readiness.

## 14. Core Risks

Key risks include:

- premature national-scale claims;
- unclear regulatory or institutional mandate;
- unsafe assumptions about remote control;
- insufficient cybersecurity or data governance;
- weak evidence for benefits or economics;
- vendor lock-in;
- lack of pilot-site readiness;
- stakeholder misalignment;
- founder-dependent execution;
- documentation that describes target architecture but not current reality.
