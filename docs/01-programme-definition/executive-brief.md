## 1. Purpose

This executive brief introduces the proposed **National Energy Operating System Programme** for government, public-sector, utility, regulatory, development-finance, and strategic technical stakeholders.

The programme proposes an open, governed digital coordination layer for distributed energy resources and energy-sector institutions. Its purpose is to help Kenya prepare for a more distributed, digital, flexible, and market-aware electricity system.

This document does not claim that a national system is already approved, procured, deployed, or operational. It defines the proposed direction and the questions that require official validation.

## 2. The Core Issue

Kenya's electricity system is moving into a more complex operating environment. Distributed solar, storage, electric mobility, net metering, open access, regional electricity trade, smart metering, and future flexibility markets increase the number of assets, actors, measurements, permissions, operating limits, contracts, and settlement records that must be coordinated.

The challenge is not only generation capacity. It is the ability to coordinate a growing ecosystem of distributed energy resources safely, securely, transparently, and within legitimate institutional authority.

The core problem is:

> Kenya does not yet have a complete, nationally governed, interoperable, secure, and operationally accepted capability for coordinating eligible distributed energy resources across local control, system operation, network constraints, market participation, metering, data exchange, and settlement processes.

## 3. What Is Proposed

The proposed programme would develop the foundations for a **National Energy Operating System** that can evolve through staged proof:

```text
Facility Energy Management System
  ↓
Regional Distributed Energy Resource Management System
  ↓
National Distributed Energy Resource Management System
  ↓
National Energy Operating System
```

The programme begins at the facility level because a facility is the smallest complete energy system where meters, generation, storage, backup supply, EV charging, controllable loads, controls, operators, costs, and reliability needs can be observed and coordinated.

After facility capability is proven, the same coordination principles can be extended to portfolios, regions, distribution domains, and eventually national visibility and coordination.

## 4. What the Programme Is Not

The programme is not intended to replace:

- the energy regulator;
- transmission or distribution utilities;
- the national control centre;
- licensed market institutions;
- asset owners;
- equipment manufacturers;
- engineering, procurement, and construction contractors;
- certified protection and control systems.

The programme is also not a proposal for unrestricted peer-to-peer trading, uncontrolled dispatch, or centralized control of private energy assets.

The proposed platform must operate only within explicit legal, regulatory, contractual, grid-code, and delegated authority.

## 5. Strategic Value

If validated and implemented correctly, the programme may support:

- improved visibility of distributed energy resources;
- safer integration of solar, storage, EV charging, and flexible demand;
- better coordination between facilities, utilities, operators, regulators, and markets;
- stronger metering, reconciliation, audit, and settlement evidence;
- improved planning for distribution-level constraints and flexibility;
- reduced fragmentation across vendor platforms and institutional systems;
- cybersecurity and data-governance discipline for energy digital infrastructure;
- a practical pathway from local energy management to regional and national coordination;
- long-term foundations for energy-market modernization and regional energy integration.

These benefits remain hypotheses until validated by data, pilots, cost-benefit analysis, stakeholder acceptance, and operating evidence.

## 6. Guiding Principles

### Coordinate globally; control locally

Higher-level systems should coordinate objectives, constraints, and verified capability. Local systems must retain authority over safety, physical limits, and site-specific operating conditions.

### Authority must be explicit

Technical ability to observe or control an asset does not create legal, commercial, regulatory, or operational authority.

### Scale follows evidence

The programme should advance only after the current level has demonstrated technical capability, operational reliability, safety, cybersecurity, user value, economics, and institutional readiness.

### Open infrastructure over proprietary dependence

The programme should prefer open standards, interoperable interfaces, portable data, and vendor-neutral architecture wherever practical.

### National readiness requires institutional readiness

National-scale digital energy infrastructure requires governance, funding, assurance, accountability, cybersecurity, procurement discipline, operating capacity, and legal clarity.

## 7. Proposed Development Path

| Stage | Objective | Evidence Required |
|---|---|---|
| Laboratory proof | Demonstrate safe coordination of heterogeneous devices in a controlled setting. | Device integration, telemetry quality, control safety, failure handling. |
| Facility EMS pilot | Deploy in one real facility and prove operational value. | Baseline, uptime, cost/reliability impact, operator acceptance, safety evidence. |
| Repeatable Facility EMS | Convert pilot capability into a repeatable product and service. | Standard deployment, support, unit economics, documented operations. |
| Portfolio aggregation | Coordinate multiple facilities under one ownership or operating domain. | Multi-site visibility, permissions, aggregation, local autonomy. |
| Regional DERMS prototype | Coordinate distributed facility capability against a regional objective. | Forecasting, dispatch requests, verification, constraints, governance. |
| Regional pilot | Validate in a bounded distribution, mini-grid, or regional operating environment. | Institutional authorization, operational integration, measurable value. |
| National architecture validation | Define national interfaces, authority, data, market, and assurance model. | Legal review, regulatory mapping, system integration plan, cost-benefit case. |

## 8. Government Review Questions

Government and public-sector stakeholders should help determine:

1. Which national energy-policy objectives this programme should support.
2. Which institutions have mandate over each proposed capability.
3. Which functions require licensing, regulatory approval, grid-code compliance, or formal delegation.
4. Which public or private pilot environments are appropriate.
5. What data, cybersecurity, procurement, and public-interest safeguards are mandatory.
6. What evidence is required before further public support, pilot approval, or national-scale consideration.
7. Whether the programme should be developed through an existing institution, public-private partnership, utility-led model, research programme, or other formal arrangement.

## 9. Immediate Recommendation

The immediate recommendation is to proceed with a structured **Discovery and Pilot Readiness Phase**.

This phase should produce:

- validated problem statement;
- stakeholder and authority map;
- regulatory and legal requirements register;
- candidate pilot-site assessment;
- Facility EMS pilot design;
- baseline measurement plan;
- cybersecurity and data-governance requirements;
- pilot budget and resourcing plan;
- decision gate for whether to proceed to implementation.

## 10. Decision Requested

The next official decision is not national deployment.

The appropriate next decision is whether relevant stakeholders support a controlled discovery and pilot-readiness process to determine if the programme is technically, institutionally, legally, economically, and operationally viable.
